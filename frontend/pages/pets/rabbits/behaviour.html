Rabbit Behaviour & Safe Enrichment

Rabbits express joy, curiosity, comfort and concern through their movements, sounds and everyday habits. Learn to read their signals and create a home where your companion can behave like a rabbit.

Get to know your rabbit

Understanding Rabbit Behaviour

Rabbits are alert, expressive prey animals. Their posture, movement and sounds offer clues about how they are feeling. Always consider the whole situation and what is normal for your individual rabbit.

🤸

Binkies & zoomies

A binky is a joyful leap or twist in the air. Zoomies are sudden bursts of running. These often appear when a rabbit feels energetic, playful and comfortable.

Often a happy sign
😴

The relaxed flop

A rabbit may suddenly roll onto its side and stretch out. It can look dramatic, but is commonly a sign of relaxation when the rabbit otherwise seems well.

Rest & comfort
🦷

Tooth purring vs. grinding

Very soft tooth-clicking while relaxed can signal contentment. Loud, persistent grinding—especially with a hunched posture or reduced appetite—can indicate pain and needs veterinary attention.

Listen to the difference
🐾

Thumping & hiding

A forceful hind-foot thump can be an alarm signal. Hiding, freezing or staying unusually still may mean a rabbit feels frightened, stressed or unwell.

Check the surroundings
Read the little signals

What is your rabbit telling you?

Look at ears, eyes, body position and context together. A single signal does not tell the whole story.

👂

Forward or moving ears

Often means your rabbit is listening or investigating something. Ears angled back can have different meanings depending on whether the body is relaxed or tense.

🧘

Loose, stretched posture

A relaxed rabbit may stretch out, sit comfortably or rest with its feet tucked under its body.

🫣

Crouching or freezing

A tightly crouched body, wide eyes or remaining motionless can be signs of fear. Give your rabbit space and reduce the source of stress.

💗

Nudging & grooming

A gentle nose nudge may be a request for attention or a way to investigate. Mutual grooming can be an important social bonding behaviour.

Remember: Rabbits are individuals. Learn your rabbit’s usual routine and behaviour so you can notice changes early.
Let them be rabbits

Natural Activities

In the wild, rabbits spend much of their day moving, searching for food, exploring and digging. A safe home environment should give them appropriate ways to express these natural behaviours.

⛏️

Digging

Digging is a normal instinct, not bad behaviour. Offer a supervised digging box filled with rabbit-safe shredded paper or child-safe soil without fertiliser or chemicals.

🐇

Hopping & running

Give your rabbit a roomy, secure area with a non-slip surface to hop, stretch, sprint and explore every day. Keep wires and hazards out of reach.

🌱

Exploring & foraging

Rabbits naturally search for food. Scatter part of their usual hay or measured daily greens around a safe area to encourage gentle exploration.

🏡

Create a rabbit-safe space

Provide hiding places with more than one exit, a clean litter area, constant access to fresh hay and water, and room to move. Supervise new spaces and remove anything that could be chewed dangerously.

Play, chew, discover

Play & Enrichment

Enrichment keeps curious minds and busy teeth occupied. Rotate a few safe options and let your rabbit choose what interests them—never force interaction.

🧺

Safe toys

Choose simple items made for rabbits and check them regularly for damage.

  • Untreated willow or apple-wood chews from a reputable pet supplier
  • Plain cardboard tunnels and boxes
  • Seagrass or willow balls made for rabbits
🥬

Interactive activities

Make everyday routines more interesting with gentle, food-based games.

  • Hide a few pieces of their usual greens in a forage mat
  • Place hay in more than one easy-to-reach spot
  • Offer a treat ball designed for rabbits, using their normal pellets
📦

DIY enrichment

Try simple homemade activities using clean, rabbit-safe materials.

  • Stuff hay loosely into a plain cardboard tube
  • Make a low-sided digging box with shredded paper
  • Cut safe doorways in a plain cardboard hideout
Safety first: Avoid treated or painted wood, staples, tape, string, plastic that can be chewed and swallowed, toxic plants, and small parts that may cause choking or intestinal blockage. Supervise new toys and remove damaged items.
Companionship & trust

Social Behaviour

Rabbits are social animals. Many benefit from a compatible rabbit companion, and they can also build trusting relationships with people through calm, patient interaction.

🐰

Rabbit companionship

A suitable bonded companion can provide social contact, grooming and resting together. Compatibility matters: rabbits should be properly introduced, and neutering is usually an important part of responsible bonding.

🤝

Building trust with people

Sit quietly at floor level, speak gently and let your rabbit approach. Offer suitable food by hand if they are comfortable. Avoid chasing, grabbing or picking them up unless necessary for care.

Introducing rabbits safely

Bonding can take time. Do not put unfamiliar rabbits together and leave them unsupervised.

Prepare separately

Arrange separate secure living spaces. Make sure both rabbits are healthy and discuss neutering and timing with a rabbit-savvy vet.

Go gradually

Allow them to become familiar with each other’s scent and begin short, closely supervised meetings in neutral territory.

Watch and reassess

Look for relaxed behaviour and positive interaction. If serious chasing, biting or fighting occurs, separate them safely and seek experienced bonding guidance.

Important: Never force rabbits to share a space, and do not leave them together until they are reliably bonded. Ask a rabbit-experienced rescue or veterinarian for help if introductions are difficult.
Notice changes early

Behavioural Concerns

A change in behaviour can be a response to the environment, fear or pain. Rabbits often hide signs of illness, so changes from their normal routine deserve attention.

⚠️

Possible signs of stress or fear

Watch for repeated hiding, freezing, frantic attempts to escape, persistent thumping, aggression that is new, or a tense crouched posture. Check for loud noises, unfamiliar animals, lack of hiding places or unwanted handling.

🔎

Unusual behaviour

Take note of sudden changes in appetite, drinking, droppings, movement, grooming, toileting or social interaction. A rabbit that stops behaving normally may be unwell, not simply being difficult.

🩺

When to contact a veterinarian

Contact a rabbit-savvy veterinarian promptly for reduced eating, fewer or smaller droppings, persistent tooth grinding, a hunched posture, unusual lethargy or signs of pain.

🚨

Do not wait for an emergency

If your rabbit stops eating or producing droppings, has difficulty breathing, collapses, has a swollen abdomen or seems severely unwell, seek urgent veterinary care. Do not try to diagnose or treat the cause at home.

📝

Keep a simple behaviour diary

Record changes in eating, droppings, activity and interactions, along with when they started. Photos or short videos can help your veterinarian understand what you have noticed.

A happier rabbit starts with understanding

Give your rabbit space to explore, safe ways to play, patient companionship and prompt care when something changes. Small daily choices can make a meaningful difference to their wellbeing.
